# Research brief: How to brush a cat's teeth, effectively and without getting injured

Audience: adult cat owners with no veterinary background. **Scope (coordinator, from the user):
the reader's cat is a hostile ADULT cat that resists handling.** The brief therefore puts the
weight on: adult cats that resist, slow desensitisation, low-stress handling and stop rules,
bite/scratch safety, and fallbacks for cats that never accept a brush. Kitten material is limited
to one line (section 2). Module: 30-45 min, 6-8 sections, self-check quizzes.

## 1. Why brush

### Prevalence of dental and periodontal disease
The sources give different figures. Use a range, and name the source and the age group.

| Figure | Population | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 50-90% have "some form of dental disease" | cats older than 4 years | Cornell [S3] |
| "Roughly 50% to 80%" have periodontal disease | cats over 4 years | VCA [S7] |
| "More than half" have some form of dental disease | cats over 3 years | VCA [S8] |
| "as many as 85%" thought to have some form of dental disease | cats 3 years and older | iCatCare [S2] |
| periodontal disease "an estimated 85 percent" | cats over 6 years | Cornell / Dr Carmichael [S6] |
| "As many as 70%" have dental disease | cats over 3 years | AAFP/ISFM 2022 [S17] |
| "up to 90% of pets are affected" (periodontal disease) | cats and dogs together | WSAVA committee [S14] |
| only "about 10 percent" go through life with no dental problem | cats | Cornell / Dr Carmichael [S6] |

- Periodontal disease is "the most common medical issue in cats and dogs" [S14].
- Periodontal disease is "by far, the most common problem associated with bad breath" [S4].
- Dental disease "becomes more common, and severe, as cats get older" [S2].

### Plaque -> tartar -> gum disease
- Plaque is "a soft film of bacteria, saliva and food that develops on the surface of the teeth"
  [S2]. VOHC: "The soft, bacteria-rich layer that rapidly forms on the surface of the teeth" [S12].
- In a healthy mouth, plaque stays above the gumline. If it is not removed, it moves down below
  the gumline, where the immune response causes gingivitis [S3].
- Plaque hardens with minerals from saliva into calculus (tartar) [S2, S3]. Tartar is "resistant
  to removal by chewing or brushing" [S12]. It "can't be removed by brushing your cat's teeth";
  a scale and polish under anaesthetic is usually necessary [S2].
- Tartar has a rough surface that plaque sticks to more easily [S8, S12]. Cornell: the bacteria
  on the calculus cause the inflammation, not the calculus itself [S3].
- **How fast plaque mineralises:** "without daily brushing, plaque quickly builds and eventually
  mineralizes (over 36-48 hours), forming hard tartar" [S8]. A second VCA page: "if it is not
  removed within 48 hours, it begins to harden into tartar" [S9]. After a professional cleaning,
  plaque returns "within days" without brushing [S4]. The primary research behind the 36-48 h
  figure was **not researched**.
- Gingivitis is "usually reversible" [S3]. Mild gingivitis: toothbrushing "can easily reverse most
  cases" [S2]. Severe gingivitis: "toothbrushing won't help"; scale and polish under general
  anaesthetic is necessary [S2].
- Periodontitis is "almost always the result of untreated gingivitis" and "eventually cannot be
  reversed" [S3]. It can lead to loose teeth and tooth loss [S3, S8].
- Advanced disease can cause oronasal fistula, jaw fracture and abscesses [S8]. "Some studies
  indicate" that bacteria from severe oral disease may be associated with changes in heart,
  liver and kidney [S8]. That evidence was **not researched**. Present it as "some studies
  suggest", not as fact.

### Gingivitis signs and bad breath
- Gingivitis signs: swelling, redness, discomfort and, in severe cases, bleeding at the gingival
  margin. The cat may hesitate to eat, turn its head while eating, stop eating, drool, have bad
  breath, or prefer soft food [S3].
- Bad breath: periodontal disease is the most common cause [S4]. Other causes include kidney
  disease (urine/ammonia smell), diabetes (sweet, fruity breath) and liver disease (foul smell
  with vomiting) [S4]. Persistent bad breath needs a vet check [S4]. So bad breath is not always
  a tooth problem.

### Tooth resorption (TR, formerly FORL)
- TR breaks down tooth structure, starting inside the tooth [S3]. Former names: feline
  odontoclastic resorptive lesion (FORL), cervical line lesion [S5].
- Prevalence figures disagree (see Disagreements D9): 20-60% of all cats and "close to
  three-quarters" of cats 5 years and older [S5]; 30-70% [S3]; about 50% [S6]; "more than 70% of
  cats over the age of five years" have at least one [S2].
- Cause: "not known" [S3]; "unknown" [S5]. VCA: unknown, "poor oral hygiene can play a role" [S8].
  An older Cornell article calls the lesions "plaque-caused" [S6] (see D9).
- **Does brushing prevent TR?** No fetched source says that brushing prevents TR. Because the
  cause is unknown [S3, S5], the module must not claim that brushing prevents it. It may say that
  brushing targets plaque and gingivitis, and that TR needs a vet diagnosis (X-rays, probing under
  anaesthesia) [S2, S5]. A direct statement such as "brushing does not prevent TR" is
  **unsourced**. Phrase it as "there is no evidence in our sources that brushing prevents TR".
- TR is often first seen as a pinkish defect at the gumline. "By the time a defect like this shows
  up, the tooth is already significantly damaged" [S3]. TR "may or may not be associated with
  gingivitis" [S3].
- TR can be very painful [S3, S2]. Behaviour signs: tilting the head and chewing on one side,
  swallowing kibble without chewing, food falling out of the mouth, sudden preference for soft
  food [S5].

### Brushing as the gold standard
- VOHC: "Excellent oral health is maintained by daily oral hygiene. The gold standard is
  brushing." [S11]
- iCatCare: brushing is "the best way to keep their teeth and gums healthy" [S1]. iCatCare also
  links its brushing article as "gold standard dental care at home" [S2].
- VCA: "The most effective way to reduce plaque and tartar is to brush your cat's teeth daily" [S8].
- Cornell: "The best way to prevent gingivitis in cats is to regularly remove plaque build-up by
  tooth brushing" [S3]. Figure caption: "Daily toothbrushing can prevent most forms of dental
  disease" [S3].
- AVDC recommends "daily dental home care", including "brushing or use of other
  effective techniques to retard accumulation of dental plaque, such as dental diets and chew
  materials" [S10].

## 2. Feline mouth basics

- Adult cats have 30 permanent teeth [S6]. (Kitten line, the only one in this brief: kittens have
  26 milk teeth, replaced by about 6 months [S6].)
- The dental formula (incisors, canines, premolars, molars per jaw) was **not researched**.
- **Which teeth matter most:**
  - VCA: concentrate on "the large cheek teeth and the canine teeth at first — the teeth where
    plaque and tartar accumulate most quickly" [S7].
  - iCatCare: start with the back teeth, "the most important to brush!" [S1].
  - The claim that the *upper* premolars and molars specifically matter most is **unsourced** in
    the fetched pages. Use "big cheek teeth at the back" [S7] unless more research is done.
- **Where plaque collects:** the gingival margin, where gum meets tooth. "This is the region where
  plaque accumulates and gingivitis is initiated" [S7]. The gumline is "the most important area of
  the tooth to clean" [S14]. Cornell aims the bristles at "the narrow crevice between teeth and
  gums" [S3].
- **Outer (cheek-side) surfaces vs inner (tongue-side) surfaces:**
  - "It's only necessary to brush the outside surfaces, as the cat's own tongue cleans the inside
    surfaces well." [S3]
  - "Most periodontal damage occurs on the outer surfaces of the teeth and around the roots ...
    The cat's abrasive tongue tends to remove plaque from the inner surfaces" [S7].
  - The WSAVA committee disagrees in part: "the inside of the teeth should be brushed if possible"
    [S14]. See D6.
- Vets examine the cheek-side (buccal) surfaces by lifting the lips before they open the mouth
  [S17]. The owner does not need to open the mouth: "The mouth can remain closed" [S7]; "Never
  force your pet's mouth open" [S9].
- Breeds and anatomy: misaligned teeth collect more plaque. Short-nosed breeds (Persian,
  Chinchilla, British and Exotic Shorthair) and retained deciduous teeth are risk factors [S2].
  Dr Carmichael names Abyssinian, Siamese, Maine Coon, Persian and Somali as higher risk [S6].

## 3. When NOT to start: see a vet first

**Signs of a painful or diseased mouth** (combined list):
- bad breath; red, sore or bleeding gums; reduced appetite; weight loss; pawing at the mouth;
  drooling; difficulty eating; visible tartar; loose teeth; reduced grooming [S2]
- head shaking, jaw chattering, chewing with obvious discomfort, dropping food, swallowing with
  difficulty, blood in saliva, refusing dry food or swallowing it whole, preference for wet food
  [S8]
- turning the head while eating, preference for soft food, irritability [S3]
- chewing on one side only, food falling from the mouth [S5]
- refusal to eat hard food [S6]
- broken teeth: teeth fractured into dentine or pulp "are likely to need extraction" because they
  are painful and can become infected [S2]

**Cats hide pain.** "Cats will often hide signs of pain, so it's also not uncommon for them to show
no signs yet have severe dental disease" [S2]. "Many cats do not display signs of dental disease
that their owners can detect" [S8]. So a vet check before you start is sensible even if the mouth
looks fine. Exam frequency: at least every 12 months; every 3-6 months for cats with dental
problems [S2].

**Gingivitis or tartar already present:**
- "If a cat has severe gingivitis, tooth brushing can be quite painful, so consult with a
  veterinarian before considering brushing the teeth of a cat with gingivitis." [S3]
- "If you see evidence of tartar accumulation or gingivitis, or your cat exhibits signs of mouth
  pain or discomfort, you should take the cat to your veterinarian" [S8].
- Brushing cannot remove tartar [S2, S12]. WSAVA committee: start brushing "following a
  professional dental cleaning" (see D4) [S14]. iCatCare: in older cats, home
  care often starts after a scale and polish, and "allow time for the mouth to heal before starting
  any kind of home care" [S1].
- Do not scrape tartar off at home with metal tools. You can harm the cat, the cat can harm you,
  and scratches on the enamel speed up plaque build-up [S8].

**A hostile adult cat: rule out pain first.** This is the key point for this reader.
- Irritability is a listed sign of oral pain [S3] and of tooth resorption [S5]. Dental disease
  "can cause serious pain and discomfort" [S3]. TR lesions are "extremely sensitive" [S2].
- AAFP/ISFM: "be aware of the high potential for oral pain" before any mouth examination. Pain is
  part of the same emotional system as fear-anxiety, and painful or fearful experiences become
  linked to the context [S17]. Frustration and fear can lead to growling, scratching and biting
  [S17].
- AAFP/ISFM: "the possibility of a range of other sources of acute or chronic pain should always
  be considered if a cat is presented as being challenging to handle"; periodontal disease is one
  of the "common chronic pain conditions in cats" [S17].
- So a cat that hisses or swats when its mouth is touched may be in pain, not only "difficult".
  The module should tell the reader to get a vet exam before training starts. This is an inference
  that joins [S3, S5, S17]; no source says it in one sentence about home toothbrushing.
- Adult cats can learn, but slowly. Brushing "can be introduced to your cat at any age, as long as
  it's done gradually, and carefully"; "older cats will just need a little more time and patience"
  [S1]. "If you have an older cat, the process may take a little longer, but is still worth the
  effort" [S7].
- AAFP/ISFM (vet context): "An adverse experience ... such as tight restraint or triggering of
  pain or fear, can lead to the cat becoming highly reactive at future visits." For a cat that
  was not well socialised to humans, "it may only take a single negative experience" to make it
  wary [S17]. For a hostile adult cat, each forced session can make the next one harder.
  (Application to home care is an inference.)
- **Do not train a distressed cat alone.** AAFP/ISFM: "Caregivers should not be tasked with
  trying to educate cats who are showing signs of distress or have existing health or behavior
  problems. In cats with problem behaviors, cooperative care education programs should only be
  performed with the input of a suitably qualified feline behaviorist." [S17] This is the
  strongest sourced statement for this reader. The module should present it plainly.

## 4. Supplies

### Toothpaste
- Use cat (veterinary) toothpaste only [S1, S3, S7, S8].
- Why not human toothpaste: the sources give different reasons.
  - Cornell: "human products can be toxic to cats" [S3]; "Never use toothpaste for humans" [S4].
  - iCatCare: "It's not safe to use human dental products for your cat"; human toothpaste is
    "unpalatable for cats and may cause irritation to the stomach" [S1].
  - VCA: human toothpastes "contain ingredients that should not be swallowed" and can upset the
    stomach. "Some human toothpastes contain high levels of sodium" [S7]. "Human toothpastes are
    foaming products and contain ingredients such as fluoride that should not be swallowed. Use
    pet toothpaste that is non-foaming and safe to be swallowed" [S8].
  - Baking soda: high alkaline content, can upset the stomach, tastes bad [S7].
- **Xylitol in cats: not researched.** None of the fetched veterinary pages mention xylitol. Do not
  state that xylitol is toxic to cats, or that it is safe, until a source is found. Xylitol
  toxicity is well documented in dogs. That is outside this brief and unsourced here.
- Flavours: chicken, beef or fish; try different ones [S1]. "Several different flavors that are
  appetizing to cats" [S7]. The claim about malt flavour is **unsourced**.
- Cat toothpaste is a gel or paste that the cat swallows. No rinsing is mentioned in any source.
  The question of whether to use toothpaste vs. a gel is **not researched**.

### Brushes and applicators
- Cat toothbrush [S3, S1]. Brushes with angled handles, small hand-held brushes, finger brushes; a
  very soft human baby toothbrush for some cats; gauze wrapped around a finger; a cotton swab [S7].
  A child's toothbrush is "an ideal alternative as the bristles are soft" [S14].
- Prefer a brush whose bristles reach just below the gumline [S7].
- It is "easy to accidentally poke the tip of the toothbrush against the gums" [S7].
- **Finger brushes: sources disagree (D2).** iCatCare: use "with caution, and probably to avoid in
  most cases, to reduce the risk of being accidentally bitten!" [S1]. VCA and WSAVA list them as
  options [S7, S9, S14].
- One brush per cat [S1, S7]. Replace every 3 months, rinse after use [S7].
- Hygiene: wear gloves if possible, or wash your hands with soap and water afterwards [S7].
- Treats/rewards: an edible treat or "a drink from her favorite water faucet" [S3]. Tuna water on
  a cotton swab (no cleaning effect, but a positive association) [S7, S8]. Tuna juice or chicken
  stock on the brush [S14].

### VOHC seal
- The Veterinary Oral Health Council (VOHC) awards a seal for "Helps Control Plaque" and/or "Helps
  Control Tartar" [S12].
- "The VOHC does not test products itself." It reviews data that the product sponsor submits
  [S11]. The sponsor funds the trials [S12]. Two separate trials are required [S12]. The council
  has nine veterinary dentists and dental scientists [S11]. Seal approval involves VOHC and the
  AVDC Board [S12].
- VOHC claim: "Regular use of products awarded the VOHC seal of acceptance will reduce the
  severity of periodontal disease in pets." [S11]
- The numerical "minimum VOHC standard" (percent reduction) is **not researched** [S12 refers to
  it but the value was not captured].
- Cat list, last updated August 2026 [S13]: dental diets (Hill's t/d, Science Diet Oral Care,
  Healthy Advantage Oral+, Purina PPVD DH, Royal Canin Feline Dental, Performatrin Prime Oral Care,
  Hill's Science Plan Vet Essentials Multi-Benefit + Dental), water additives, gel, spray, a
  toothpaste/brush kit, wipes (all HealthyMouth), ProDen PlaqueOff powders, and treats (Feline
  Greenies, Whiskas Dentabites, Purina DentaLife, PPVD Dental Bites). Several products have a
  plaque claim only or a tartar claim only [S13]. Do not name products in the module without a
  reason. If you do, cite the list date.
- Observation from [S13]: there is only one toothpaste product on the cat list (a kit), and no
  chlorhexidine product. Most cat toothpastes on sale therefore do not carry the seal. This is an
  inference from the list, not a VOHC statement.

## 5. Training plan (desensitisation and counter-conditioning)

**Principle.** "Desensitization and counterconditioning: breaking down the process into a series
of tiny steps and pairing each step with something pleasant. Each step should be easily accepted;
if your pet becomes concerned, take a break and start with an easier step next time." [S9]
"Remember – your pet gets to set the pace!" [S9]

**Durations.**
- Cornell: a four-week programme, one step per week [S3].
- iCatCare: "it doesn't matter how long it takes ... If it takes more than a couple of days for
  each stage, that's fine" [S1].
- WSAVA committee: "it may take a few weeks of trying each day" [S14].
- VCA: "a few minutes for some pets and a few months for others". If there is no progress after a
  few weeks, ask a behaviour professional [S9].
- VCA: build up to all teeth over "several days or weeks" [S7].

**Typical step ladders** (side by side; the order differs between sources):

| Step | Cornell [S3] | iCatCare [S1] | WSAVA committee [S14] | VCA [S7, S9] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | - | touch around head and mouth, gentle stroking, reward | touch face, stroke, lift top lip, reward | pick a quiet small room; table on a towel, or lap [S7]; reward for staying still, build up to 2 min [S9] |
| 1 | Wk 1: brush and paste left out; cat licks paste from finger; reward | paste on finger to lick; try flavours | index finger touches one top tooth (canine), add a tooth daily | cotton swab with tuna water [S7] |
| 2 | Wk 2: dab of paste on a canine tooth daily; reward | practise the head hold when relaxed, at other times than brushing | massage the gumline (finger brush allowed); taste paste first | lift lips, mouth closed; rub the gingival margin of a few teeth [S7] |
| 3 | Wk 3: lick paste off the brush; reward | cotton bud with paste, circular motion, back teeth first | change to toothbrush; go back to one tooth at a time | brush with lickable treat slid onto front teeth [S9] |
| 4 | Wk 4: lips stretched, brush at 45 degrees into the gum crevice | toothbrush, back teeth first, 10 s then 30 s per side | gumline with bristles; inner surfaces only if allowed | paste on brush, brush 1-2 s, treat, repeat [S9] |

**Session length and timing.**
- "A typical session might only be 30 seconds to start." [S9]
- Full brushing takes "less than 2 minutes" [S14].
- Timing: after a meal, or with food as a reward: "There's no right or wrong" [S1]. Mealtime,
  before bed, or first thing in the morning [S14]. "Choose a quiet time and place" [S7]. Practise
  the head hold "at a different time to when you plan to brush" [S1].
- Brushing after play is **unsourced**. Brushing before a meal is **unsourced** (iCatCare says
  either way [S1]).
- Routine: "cats like their routine" [S1]. "Habits are hard to start, but easy to break" [S14].

**Adapting the ladder for a resistant or hostile adult cat.**
- Get a vet exam first, to find or treat oral pain (section 3) [S3, S17]. Ask for behaviourist
  input if the cat shows problem behaviours [S17].
- Expect the long end of the range: weeks to months, not days [S1, S9]. "Older cats will just
  need a little more time and patience" [S1]. If there is no progress "after a few weeks, seek
  professional guidance from a behavior consultant or trainer skilled in teaching toothbrushing"
  [S9].
- Start further down the ladder than the step lists show. Step 0 is touch the cat already likes.
  AAFP/ISFM: the preferred areas of touch are the head and neck, especially the facial glands
  [S17]. iCatCare starts with "gentle stroking" around the head and mouth [S1].
- Use reinforcement of small approximations. AAFP/ISFM "cooperative care": the wanted behaviour
  "is not initially automatically offered by the cat and so training involves the successive
  reinforcement of approximations". Approximations of the equipment can be used first [S17].
  "Caregivers can use positive reinforcement training at home to teach their cats to enjoy
  handling" like that in a vet exam [S17].
- Give the cat control. AAFP/ISFM: give a sense of control and choice; "Releasing the cat must
  always be an option" [S17]. Confident cats "may be happy to initiate a short amount of contact
  ... but may not be happy with prolonged intense contact" [S17]. Keep sessions very short (VCA:
  ~30 s to start [S9]).
- Use high-value food. Treats engage positive emotions and can distract some cats during handling
  [S17]. Lickable treats work while the brush is in place [S9]. Tuna water or chicken stock can be
  used on the swab or brush [S7, S14].
- Practise the head hold when the cat is relaxed, and at a different time from brushing [S1].
- One step at a time; go back a step after any setback [S9, S14]. "Things may take a backward
  step when you first introduce the toothbrush" [S14].
- The precise ladder for a *hostile* cat (for example, rewarding the cat for looking at the
  brush across the room) is **unsourced** as a toothbrushing protocol. It follows the
  approximation principle in [S17] and [S9]. A behaviourist should design it [S17].
- Sedation or anxiolytics *for home toothbrushing*: **unsourced / not researched**. AAFP/ISFM
  discusses pre-visit anxiolytics (for example gabapentin) for **vet visits** only. These are
  prescription drugs and the guideline describes them for that context [S17]. The module may say
  "ask your vet whether anti-anxiety medication would help with vet visits". It must not suggest
  drugs or doses for home brushing.

**Stop rules.**
- "Stop if your cat shows signs of being uncomfortable and try again the next day." [S1]
- "If you feel your pet is too uncomfortable, stop there and try again the next day." [S14]
- "Always end on a positive note with a reward" [S1].
- iCatCare: home care should not be "a battle between you or something they find stressful" [S1].

## 6. Technique

- **Position.**
  - iCatCare: "Position your cat with their back towards you - this is less confrontational for
    your cat, and if they wriggle, it tends to be backwards, so you'll have better control." [S1]
  - VCA: on a counter or tabletop (on a blanket or towel), or in your lap. Use a small room with
    the door closed "to seal off escape avenues" [S7]. A low table with a towel for traction [S9].
  - VCA: train the cat to rest its chin on your palm, to steady the head [S9].
  - A "side approach" and "do not restrain the head from the front" are **unsourced** as stated.
    The closest sources are iCatCare's back-towards-you position [S1] and AAFP/ISFM guidance
    against restraint [S17].
- **Head hold (iCatCare):** palm on top of the head, towards the back. Thumb and second finger
  gently around the cheekbones below the eyes. Index finger lifted, so that it does not cover the
  eyes. Tilt the head up a little. Lift the upper lip with the thumb. Pull the lower lip down with
  the other hand [S1].
- **Lift the lip; keep the mouth closed.** "Gently pull back her lips. The mouth can remain
  closed." [S7] "Gently stretch back the lips rather than forcing the mouth open" [S4].
- **Angle.** Cornell: "Place the bristles at a 45 degree angle to the teeth, aiming for the narrow
  crevice between teeth and gums." [S3] Note: VCA's "45-degree angle" refers to the *cat's head*,
  not the brush [S7]. Do not mix up the two.
- **Motion.** "Small, circular motions" [S1]. "Brush around gently" [S3]. "Gentle massage along
  the gumline" [S14].
- **Surfaces.** Outer surfaces and gumline [S3, S7]. Inner surfaces only if the cat allows [S14].
  See D6.
- **Order.** iCatCare: start at the back teeth and work forward [S1]. VCA: cheek teeth and canines
  first [S7]. VCA training article: front teeth first while training [S9]. Cornell and WSAVA: the
  canine is the first tooth touched [S3, S14]. Sources disagree (D5). "Front to back" is supported
  only for the *training* order [S3, S9, S14], not for the brushing order.
- **Duration.** "Approximately 30 seconds per side" [S7]. "Start with just ten seconds on each
  side, gradually building up to 30 seconds on each side" [S1].
- **Bleeding.** "At first, you may notice a small amount of bleeding from the gums; this is
  common and will stop as you brush more regularly" [S1]. If the gums "bleed more than usual
  during brushing", tell your vet [S9]. Severe gingivitis: see a vet before brushing [S3].

## 7. Safety: not getting injured

### Stress signals (body language)
From the VCA training article (dogs and cats; cat-specific items marked) [S9]:
- moving the head away; holding the lips tight; dilated pupils
- ears held back (cats); ears folded to the side or back
- tail twitching (cats)
- leaning away, looking away, trying to flee; retreating when the brush appears
- becoming tense or frozen; trembling
- vocalising (growl, hiss); swatting, biting

From AAFP/ISFM 2022 [S17]:
- Three response types to fear: **inhibition** (passive, "often not recognized"; example: eyes
  half-shut and ears rotated laterally), **avoidance** (escape), **repelling** (growling, hissing,
  striking, biting).
- Splayed whiskers are a sign of protective (negative) emotion.
- "An inhibited cat who is freezing can become more aroused if the cause of the fear is not
  addressed". A cat whose inhibition does not work may move on to avoidance or repelling.
- Frustration (for example from tight restraint) "leads to more intense and rapid behavioral
  responses", often growling, scratching or biting.
- Relaxed or engaging body language: ears up and forward, head and body upright, tail up, relaxed
  lying position, exploring.

**Unsourced in this brief:** skin twitching / rippling, tail thumping, flattened (as distinct
from "back") ears. Lip-licking appears in [S17] only as a sign of motion sickness (with drooling
and vomiting), not as a stress signal; do not list it as a stress signal without a new source.
Fear Free and Cat Friendly Homes owner pages were **not researched**.

**For a hostile cat, the key is to stop early.** In the AAFP/ISFM model, a cat whose quiet
signals (inhibition, avoidance) do not work moves on to repelling: growl, hiss, strike, bite
[S17]. So the owner should stop at the *quiet* signals (freezing, ears turning, head moving away,
lips held tight) [S9, S17] and not wait for the hiss or the swat. This is a direct reading of the
model in [S17], applied to home care.

### When to stop
- Stop at signs of discomfort and try the next day [S1, S14]. Take a break and go back to an
  easier step [S9].
- AAFP/ISFM (vet setting, applies by analogy): "Releasing the cat must always be an option";
  "slow down when required, and give the cat breaks" [S17].

### Low-stress handling and scruffing
- **Current AAFP/ISFM position (2022):** "scruffing is no longer recommended for cats at any life
  stage. It is a mistaken premise that scruffing makes patients easier to work with. Scruffing
  causes negative responses that impact on future visits, as the cat will remember the painful or
  stressful event." [S17]
- One study cited by the guideline: full-body, clip and scruff restraint all increased fear and
  aversion compared with minimal handling [S17].
- "Working cooperatively with cats increases human safety ... Restraint increases negative
  emotions and undesirable behavioral responses." [S17]
- The guideline is for veterinary teams. Applying it to home toothbrushing is a reasonable
  extension, but no source makes that link explicitly.

### Towel wrap ("burrito")
- AAFP/ISFM: towels and blankets "to loosely wrap (rather than tightly restrain)"; "gently wrap –
  but not to tightly restrain" [S17]. A loose towel gives a sense of security [S17].
- The guideline also notes that "tight restraint" can trigger frustration [S17].
- A towel wrap *specifically for home toothbrushing* is **unsourced**. VCA suggests a blanket or
  towel on the table surface [S7], not a wrap.

### Fingers and bites
- iCatCare avoids finger brushes because of the bite risk [S1].
- The WSAVA committee suggests a finger "behind the lower canine" to get the pet to open its mouth
  for inner-surface brushing [S14]. This guidance is for dogs and cats together. For cats it puts
  fingers inside the mouth; see D2/D6.
- "Keep fingers away from the back of the mouth" as an explicit rule is **unsourced**.
- For a hostile cat, the sourced choices that keep fingers out of the mouth are: a handled brush
  rather than a finger brush [S1]; the mouth stays closed and only the lips are lifted [S7]; never
  force the mouth open [S9].
- Gloves: VCA advises gloves "if possible", but the stated reason is the bacteria in the cat's
  mouth, not bite protection [S7]. Whether gloves protect against bites is **unsourced**.
- The AVDC notes that a person working on an awake pet's mouth "may be bitten when the patient
  reacts" (in the context of scaling) [S10].

### If you are bitten or scratched
These sources were seen only as search snippets (**unverified**). Check them before use:
- Mayo Clinic study (Babovic, Cayci, Carlsen; J Hand Surg 2014): retrospective review of 193
  patients treated for cat bites to the hand, 2009-2011. About 30% were admitted (mean stay 3.2
  days). Of admitted patients, 67% (38) had irrigation and debridement, and 8 needed more than one
  operation. A bite over a joint or tendon sheath was a risk factor for admission [S20].
  **Caveat:** these are people who came to hospital, not all people bitten. A blog critique makes
  this point [S24]. Media turned the result into "1 in 3 bitten are hospitalised" [S24].
- CDC EID article (2019): 28%-80% of cat bites become infected; 75% of cat bites are associated
  with *Pasteurella multocida* [S22].
- CDC (Capnocytophaga page): after a dog or cat bite, wash the area at once with soap and water,
  then call your doctor or go to urgent care [S23].
- CDC cat scratch disease (*Bartonella henselae*): avoid scratches, bites and licks, especially
  from kittens or strays; wash hands after handling cats; wash scratches with soap and water; ask a vet about
  flea control. Higher risk for people with weakened immune systems [S21].
- **Not researched:** Mayo Clinic patient pages, NHS animal-bite page, rabies guidance.

## 8. Frequency, maintenance and alternatives

### Frequency
- Daily: iCatCare ("once daily is ideal") [S1]; Cornell [S3, S4]; VCA ("Daily brushing is most
  beneficial") [S7]; WSAVA committee ("You only need brush your pet's teeth once each day") [S14];
  AVDC (daily home care) [S10]; VOHC (daily oral hygiene) [S11].
- Minimum: "Brushing three times a week is the minimum recommendation" [S7]. See D1.
- Evidence comparing daily brushing with less frequent brushing: **not researched**.

### What success looks like
- Brushing is "a pleasant experience for your cat", not a battle [S1]. Initial gum bleeding stops
  as the gums get healthier [S1].
- Brushing also gives "a chance to detect potentially serious changes" (bleeding, odour) [S9].
- Objective success measures (gingivitis scores, etc.) for owners: **not researched**.

### Alternatives for cats that never accept brushing
- iCatCare: "Some cats simply won't tolerate having their teeth brushed". Dental chews "help to
  reduce (but not prevent)" plaque and tartar. Put toothpaste on dry food or chews. A dental diet
  is "a good option if your cat won't tolerate having their teeth brushed" [S1].
- VCA: water additives, treats and kibble diets "can reduce tartar accumulation"; check VOHC [S8].
- VOHC: "Daily chewing activities can also be effective"; daily use of VOHC-seal products helps
  [S11].
- AVDC counts dental diets and chew materials as "other effective techniques" [S10].
- **Strength of evidence:** the VOHC seal means two sponsor-funded trials met the VOHC standard
  [S11, S12]. VOHC does not run the trials itself [S11]. Head-to-head comparisons of these products
  with brushing: **not researched**. Gels and water additives on the cat list carry only a
  plaque claim [S13].
- Diet in general: "Diet may play a role in dental disease in cats, but the relationship is not
  clear" [S2]. See D7.

### Plan B for a cat that never accepts a brush (ordered from least to most effort for the cat)
1. Ask the vet team for help. Clinics may offer a vet nurse appointment to demonstrate brushing
   [S1]. Ask for a behaviour consultant if there is no progress after weeks [S9], or a feline
   behaviourist for a cat with problem behaviours [S17].
2. Use passive products that need no handling: VOHC-accepted dental diet, treats, water additives
   or powders [S1, S8, S13]. Toothpaste on dry food or chews, if the cat likes the taste [S1].
   Gels and sprays need some mouth contact; the cat list shows plaque claims only for them [S13].
3. More frequent vet exams: at least every 12 months, every 3-6 months for cats with dental
   problems [S2].
4. Professional cleaning under general anaesthetic when the vet advises it [S8, S10, S11]. Not
   NPDS [S10].
5. For vet visits, the vet may suggest carrier training and pre-visit anti-anxiety medication
   [S17]. This applies to the vet visit, not to home brushing.
- Present this honestly: brushing remains the gold standard [S11]. Chews "reduce (but not
  prevent)" plaque and tartar [S1]. The sources do not say how much less effective the
  alternatives are (D8).

### Professional cleaning under anaesthesia
- A scale and polish under general anaesthetic removes tartar [S2, S8]. X-rays and probing need
  anaesthesia [S3, S5].
- "A full general anesthetic is required for dentistry." Scaling a cooperative awake animal "is
  cosmetic only and does not diagnose or treat any dental disease present" [S8].
- Frequency varies by cat: "in some cases, professional cleaning may be required every 6–12
  months" [S8]. "Most pets also benefit from a professional cleaning at least once every year"
  [S9]. Annual checkups [S14]. "Many pets, particularly middle-aged and older cats and dogs,
  require periodic professional scaling in addition to on-going plaque control" [S11].

### Why non-anaesthetic "dental cleaning" is discouraged (AVDC, adopted 10 April 2004) [S10]
- AVDC uses the term **Non-Professional Dental Scaling (NPDS)** instead of "anesthesia-free
  dentistry".
- Reasons: (1) sharp instruments; "Even slight head movement by the patient could result in
  injury to the oral tissues of the patient, and the operator may be bitten". (2) "access to the
  subgingival area of every tooth is impossible in an unanesthetized canine or feline patient";
  "Removal of dental tartar on the visible surfaces of the teeth has little effect on a pet's
  health, and provides a false sense of accomplishment. The effect is purely cosmetic." (3) A
  cuffed endotracheal tube protects the airway. (4) A full oral exam is not possible awake; the
  tongue-side surfaces cannot be examined.
- "Although anesthesia will never be 100% risk-free, modern anesthetic and patient evaluation
  techniques ... minimize the risks".
- WSAVA 2026 abstract: anaesthesia-free dentistry is "ineffective at best and damaging at worst"
  [S16].

## Disagreements (recorded, not resolved)

**D1. Frequency: daily vs. 3x/week.**
Daily is ideal/most beneficial [S1, S3, S7, S10, S11, S14]. VCA also states "three times a week
is the minimum" [S7]. No fetched source gives evidence for either threshold.

**D2. Finger brushes / fingers in the mouth.**
iCatCare: avoid in most cases (bite risk) [S1]. VCA: finger brush and gauze on a finger are
options [S7]; a soft fingertip brush if the handle is bulky [S9]. WSAVA committee: index finger
on the teeth (step 2), finger-style brush (step 3), finger behind the lower canine for the inner
surfaces (step 5) [S14]. Note that S9 and S14 cover dogs and cats together.

**D3. Gauze and cotton swabs.**
VCA lists gauze wrapped on a finger and a cotton swab [S7]; tuna-water swab for training [S7, S8].
iCatCare uses a cotton bud as an intermediate step [S1] and does not mention gauze. Cornell goes
from finger to brush [S3, S4]. Comparative effectiveness: **not researched**.

**D4. Starting point.** (The age disagreement is out of scope for an adult cat.) Relevant part:
start after a professional cleaning [S14, S1] vs. start on the current mouth after a vet check
[S3, S8]. iCatCare says to let the mouth heal after a cleaning [S1].

**D5. Which teeth first.**
Back teeth first, "the most important to brush" [S1]; cheek teeth and canines first [S7]; canine
first in training [S3, S14]; front teeth first in training [S9].

**D6. Inner (tongue-side) surfaces.**
Not needed; the tongue cleans them [S3, S7, S9]. "Should be brushed if possible" [S14].

**D7. Wet vs. dry food.**
"Some veterinarians believe that dry food is better than canned" [S4] (hedged). "Diet may play a
role ... but the relationship is not clear" [S2]. Dr Carmichael blames "the mush that we feed"
[S6] (opinion in an older article). Specific VOHC dental diets are a different case from
ordinary dry food [S13]. **Wet vs. dry dental *gel/paste*: not researched.**

**D8. Diets, treats, water additives and gels as substitutes for brushing.**
Brushing is the gold standard [S11, S2]. Dental diet "a good option" if brushing is not tolerated;
chews "reduce (but not prevent)" [S1]. AVDC groups diets and chews with brushing as "other
effective techniques" [S10]. VOHC: daily use of seal products helps [S11]. The sources do not
agree on whether these are *substitutes* or only *supplements*.

**D9. Tooth resorption: prevalence and cause.**
Prevalence: 20-60% of all cats and ~75% of cats aged 5 or more [S5]; 30-70% [S3]; ~50% [S6]; >70%
of cats over 5 [S2]. Cause: unknown [S3, S5]; unknown but poor hygiene "can play a role" [S8];
"plaque-caused lesions" [S6] (older, single-clinician article). Treatment: "the only effective
treatment will entail extraction" [S5] vs. monitoring when only the root is affected and there is
no obvious discomfort [S3].

**D10. Periodontal/dental disease prevalence figures.** See the table in section 1. The figures
range from 50% to 90%, with age cut-offs of 3, 4 or 6 years [S2, S3, S6, S7, S8, S17] and "up to
90% of pets" [S14].

**D11. Periodontitis treatment.**
"Extraction is the only treatment option" at that stage [S2] vs. scaling and polishing "while
trying to save the teeth wherever possible", extraction in extreme cases [S3].

**D12. Why human toothpaste is unsafe.**
"Toxic" [S3]; unpalatable and stomach irritation [S1]; not to be swallowed, sodium [S7]; foaming
and fluoride [S8]. Xylitol: **not researched**. The sources agree on the advice, but they give
different reasons.

**D13. Chlorhexidine.** **Not researched.** No chlorhexidine product is on the VOHC cat list
(Aug 2026) [S13].

**D14. Brushing through bleeding gums.**
Some bleeding at first "is common and will stop" [S1] vs. severe gingivitis makes brushing "quite
painful", so consult a vet first [S3]. These are compatible if the module separates "a little
bleeding at the start" from "red, sore gums before you start".

**D15. Professional cleaning interval.**
As needed, sometimes every 6-12 months [S8]; "at least once every year" for most pets [S9];
exams yearly, or every 3-6 months for cats with dental problems [S2]; "periodic" [S11].

**D16. Per-session duration.**
10 s, building to 30 s per side [S1]; ~30 s per side [S7]; first sessions ~30 s total [S9]; full
brushing under 2 min [S14].

**D17. First taste: paste vs. tuna water.**
Paste from a finger [S3, S1] vs. tuna water on a swab [S7, S8] vs. tuna juice or chicken stock on
the brush [S14].

## Quiz items (10)

**Q1. How long can plaque take to start hardening into tartar if it is not removed?**
- Right: About 1-2 days (36-48 hours). VCA gives 36-48 h [S8] and "within 48 hours" [S9].
- Wrong: About 6 months. This is far too slow; the sources give hours to days [S8, S9].
- Wrong: It never hardens if the cat eats dry food. No source supports this; diet's role "is not
  clear" [S2].
- Wrong: About 2 weeks. Inconsistent with [S8, S9].

**Q2. Your cat has a yellow-brown crust on its back teeth. What does brushing do?**
- Right: It will not remove it. Tartar needs a scale and polish under anaesthetic, so see a vet
  first [S2, S12, S8].
- Wrong: Brush harder to remove it. Tartar "can't be removed by brushing" [S2]; harder brushing
  is not advised anywhere.
- Wrong: Scrape it off with a metal tool. VCA warns against this (injury, enamel scratches) [S8].
- Wrong: Book an anaesthesia-free cleaning. AVDC: cosmetic only, "a false sense of
  accomplishment" [S10].

**Q3. Which tooth surfaces matter most when you brush a cat's teeth?**
- Right: The outer (cheek-side) surfaces at the gumline, especially the big cheek teeth and the
  canines [S3, S7].
- Wrong: The biting tips. VCA: do not worry about the tips [S7].
- Wrong: The inner surfaces only. The tongue helps clean these [S3, S7]; WSAVA says inner if
  possible, but not *only* inner [S14].
- Wrong: The front incisors only. VCA points to the cheek teeth and canines [S7].

**Q4. Which toothpaste should you use?**
- Right: A toothpaste made for cats [S1, S3, S7].
- Wrong: A small amount of human toothpaste. Human products are unsafe for cats (fluoride,
  foaming, stomach irritation) [S3, S8, S1].
- Wrong: Baking soda. Alkaline, upsets the stomach, tastes bad [S7].
- Wrong: Any product, because cats rinse. Cats swallow it; pet toothpaste is made to be
  swallowed [S8].

**Q5. What is the best position for most cats?**
- Right: The cat with its back towards you (on your lap or a table), which is less confrontational
  [S1, S7].
- Wrong: Face to face, holding the head from the front. iCatCare chose back-towards-you because it
  is less confrontational [S1].
- Wrong: Scruff the cat to keep it still. AAFP/ISFM: scruffing is "no longer recommended for cats
  at any life stage" [S17].
- Wrong: Wrap the cat tightly in a towel. AAFP/ISFM: wrap loosely, not tight restraint [S17].

**Q6. During a session your cat's ears rotate sideways, it goes still, and its pupils widen. What do
you do?**
- Right: Stop or go back to an easier step, end on a reward, and try again tomorrow. These are
  stress signs [S9, S17, S1].
- Wrong: Continue, because a still cat is a calm cat. Freezing can be inhibition, which "is often
  not recognized" and can escalate [S17].
- Wrong: Hold the cat more firmly. Restraint increases negative emotions and frustration [S17].
- Wrong: Finish the whole mouth quickly. VCA: your pet sets the pace [S9].

**Q7. Is a cat with no obvious signs of mouth pain free of dental disease?**
- Right: Not necessarily. Cats often hide pain and can have severe disease with no signs [S2, S8].
- Wrong: Yes; a cat in pain always stops eating. Cats often eat until the disease is extreme [S5].
- Wrong: Yes, if the breath smells normal. Bad breath is one sign among many [S2, S8].

**Q8. Your adult cat hisses and swats whenever you touch near its mouth. What is the best first
step?**
- Right: Book a vet exam, because the cause may be oral pain, and ask about a behaviourist. Oral
  pain is common and irritability is a sign of it; vets should consider pain in cats that are hard
  to handle; distressed cats with problem behaviours should be trained only with behaviourist
  input [S3, S5, S17].
- Wrong: Hold the cat firmly and brush quickly to get it over with. Restraint increases negative
  emotions, and bad experiences make the cat more reactive [S17].
- Wrong: Scruff the cat to calm it. Not recommended at any life stage [S17].
- Wrong: Use a finger brush so you can feel what you are doing. iCatCare advises against finger
  brushes in most cases because of the bite risk [S1].
- (Frequency question moved to the candidate list: daily is ideal [S1, S3, S7, S14]; VCA gives 3x
  a week as a minimum [S7]; see D1.)

**Q9. Your cat will not accept brushing after weeks of patient training. Which option has the most
support?**
- Right: Ask your vet about VOHC-accepted dental diets, treats or other products, and keep up vet
  checks [S1, S8, S11, S13].
- Wrong: Force it, because brushing is the only thing that works. Brushing should not be a battle
  [S1], and alternatives exist [S1, S10].
- Wrong: Any "dental" product works. Only seal products have met the VOHC standard [S8, S11].
- Wrong: Give up on dental care completely. Exams and alternatives still help [S2, S11].

**Q10. Why does the AVDC discourage non-anaesthetic ("anaesthesia-free") cleaning?**
- Right: It cannot reach below the gumline, where disease is active, and it cannot examine the
  whole mouth. The effect is "purely cosmetic" [S10].
- Wrong: Because it is too expensive. AVDC does not give cost as a reason [S10].
- Wrong: Because anaesthesia has no risk. AVDC says anaesthesia "will never be 100% risk-free"
  [S10].
- Wrong: Because cats' teeth cannot be scaled. They are scaled routinely, under anaesthesia [S10,
  S8].

wash with soap and water, contact a doctor [S23]; hand bites can lead to hospital admission [S20].)

## Glossary

- **Plaque**: a soft film of bacteria, saliva and food on the teeth [S2]; "soft, bacteria-rich"
  and forms rapidly [S12].
- **Calculus / tartar**: plaque hardened with minerals from saliva; cream, yellow or brown; cannot
  be brushed off [S2, S3, S12]. VOHC uses "tartar" as the public term [S12].
- **Gingiva / gingival margin / gumline**: the gum; the line where the gum meets the tooth, where
  plaque collects and gingivitis starts [S3, S7].
- **Gingivitis**: inflammation of the gums; usually reversible [S2, S3].
- **Periodontium**: gingiva, cementum, periodontal ligament and bone around the tooth [S8].
- **Periodontitis / periodontal disease**: disease of the tooth-supporting tissues; follows
  untreated gingivitis; eventually not reversible [S3, S8].
- **Tooth resorption (TR; formerly FORL)**: breakdown of the tooth that starts inside it; cause
  unknown; painful [S3, S5].
- **Feline chronic gingivostomatitis (FCGS)**: severe inflammation that spreads from the gums to
  other parts of the mouth; very painful [S2].
- **Protective (negative) emotions**: fear-anxiety, pain and frustration in the AAFP/ISFM model
  [S17].
- **Cooperative care**: training with positive reinforcement so that the cat accepts care
  voluntarily, built from small approximations [S17].
- **Anxiolytic**: anti-anxiety medicine. AAFP/ISFM discusses them for vet visits, prescribed by a
  vet [S17].
- **Halitosis**: bad breath [S2].
- **Buccal / lingual surfaces**: cheek-side and tongue-side tooth surfaces (terms used in [S17];
  plain-language definitions are standard but unsourced here).
- **Scale and polish**: professional removal of plaque and tartar above and below the gumline,
  under general anaesthetic [S2, S10].
- **NPDS (Non-Professional Dental Scaling)**: AVDC's term for "anaesthesia-free dentistry" [S10].
- **VOHC**: Veterinary Oral Health Council; awards a seal for plaque and/or tartar control based on
  submitted trial data [S11, S12].
- **Desensitisation**: breaking a task into tiny steps that the animal easily accepts [S9].
- **Counter-conditioning**: pairing each step with something pleasant [S9].
- **Scruffing**: holding the cat by the loose skin at the back of the neck; not recommended at any
  life stage (AAFP/ISFM) [S17].
- **Inhibition / avoidance / repelling**: the three fear responses in the AAFP/ISFM model (freeze;
  escape; growl, hiss, strike, bite) [S17].
- **Pasteurella multocida**: a bacterium linked to most infected cat bites [S22, snippet only].
- **Bartonella henselae / cat scratch disease**: infection from cat scratches or bites [S21,
  snippet only].

## Gotchas and myths

- **"Dry food cleans cats' teeth."** Unproven as a general claim. iCatCare: the relationship
  between diet and dental disease "is not clear" [S2]. Some vets believe it [S4]. Specific VOHC
  dental diets are a different case [S13].
- **"Healthy-looking teeth don't need care."** Cats hide pain [S2]. Disease can be invisible to
  owners [S8]. TR can be under the gum [S2].
- **"Brushing removes tartar."** No [S2, S12].
- **"Brushing prevents tooth resorption."** No source supports this; the cause is unknown [S3, S5].
- **"A little human toothpaste is fine."** No [S3, S7, S8].
- **"Anaesthesia-free cleaning is a safe compromise."** AVDC: cosmetic only, risk of injury and
  bites [S10].
- **"Scruffing calms cats."** AAFP/ISFM: "a mistaken premise" [S17].
- **"My cat is being naughty."** AAFP/ISFM: the cat reacts from fear, pain or frustration [S17]
  (said about vet visits; it applies by analogy).
- **"Some bleeding means stop for good."** A little bleeding at first is common [S1]. But sore,
  red gums before you start need a vet check [S3].
- **"You have to open the mouth."** No; the mouth can stay closed [S7].
- **"Bad breath always means teeth."** Kidney disease, diabetes and liver disease can also cause
  it [S4].
- **"An adult cat that hates handling can never be brushed."** Partly false: any age is
  possible with a gradual approach, but it takes more time [S1, S7]. Partly true: "Some cats
  simply won't tolerate having their teeth brushed, no matter how much you persevere" [S1]. Then
  Plan B applies.
- **"If I just push through, the cat will get used to it."** AAFP/ISFM: bad experiences make cats
  more reactive later; restraint increases negative emotions [S17].
