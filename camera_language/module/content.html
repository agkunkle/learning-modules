“Make it look cool”

You know a good shot when you see one. The hard part is asking for it. When you work with an AI, it cannot see the picture in your head. It reads your words. If you write “make it more dramatic”, it must guess what you mean: closer, lower, slower or darker. It picks one, and often it is not the one you meant.

Film crews solved this problem a long time ago with a shared vocabulary. A director says “medium close-up, low angle, slow push-in”, and everyone on the set knows what to build. The same words work with Claude. They also work with an AI video generator, and with a friend who holds a phone.

This module teaches that vocabulary for directing an AI. The main example is code-rendered 3D: Claude writes code (for example three.js inside Remotion) that places a virtual camera and draws every frame. Every picture and clip on this page is made that way. A small 3D renderer inside the page draws the cast of the Applying Gravity music video: Ada, in the green hoodie, and Rosie, in the pink cardigan, who usually holds a pumpkin. They stand in a classroom with a chalkboard, a globe and a bowling-ball pendulum.

Real requests appear all through the module. They come from the review notes on Applying Gravity, written in plain words: “camera angle from the floor looking up”, “quick pan to Rosie”, “zoom shot to the spinning globe”. Each one has a precise name. By the end, you will know them all.

A shot in one line

Size + angle + move + timing. For example: “Medium close-up of Rosie, low angle, slow push-in, 3 seconds, cut on ‘gravity’.” Sections 1 to 3 teach the first three parts, section 6 teaches timing, and section 8 puts them together.

The film terms here are common practice. Facts about tools come from their official documentation, read in October 2026. A list of sources is at the end.

Size and angle

Start with the two words that describe a still frame. Shot size says how much of the subject the frame holds. Name it by where the bottom edge of the frame cuts the body. Here is Ada at seven sizes:

SizeThe frame holdsUse it for
Extreme close-up (ECU)One detail: the eyes, a hand, the stopwatch Intensity; a detail that matters
Close-up (CU)The faceEmotion; a sung line
Medium close-up (MCU)Head and chestMost talking and singing

Cowboy (medium wide)Mid-thigh upBody language, face still readable
Full shotHead to toeDance and movement
Wide, or establishing shotThe whole placeWhere we are; the start of a scene

The cowboy shot comes from westerns: it shows the holster at the hip. Sources differ on where it cuts: StudioBinder says mid-thigh, and Wikipedia's “American shot” says the knee. “Mid-thigh up” is clear to anyone.

Some shots get a name from what they show, not from their size. An insert is a closer view of something inside the scene, such as Rosie's pumpkin. A cutaway shows something outside the main action, such as the clock on the wall, and then the edit comes back. A two-shot holds two people; a single holds one.

Angle: where the camera sits

Camera angle is the camera's height and direction compared with the subject's eyes. The same girl looks different from each height:

  • Eye level is neutral. The viewer meets the subject as an equal.
  • A high angle looks down. The subject looks smaller.
  • A low angle looks up. The subject looks bigger and stronger.
  • Top-down (bird's-eye) looks straight down. It is good for patterns, choreography and objects on a table.
  • Worm's-eye is from the floor, looking steeply up. This is the “camera angle from the floor looking up” in the review notes.
  • A Dutch angle rolls the horizon. It suggests unease, chaos or wild energy.

These meanings are conventions, not rules. A low angle often reads as power, but the context decides. A low angle on a scared character in a dark corridor reads as threat from above, not as their power.

Tilt is not Dutch

In film, a tilt is a move: the camera turns up or down. A rolled horizon is a Dutch angle (or canted angle). “Tilt the camera” can give you either one. Write “Dutch angle” when you want the roll.

Whose eyes? OTS and POV

An over-the-shoulder shot (OTS) looks past one person's shoulder at the other. It keeps both people in the scene and is the standard shot for conversations. A point-of-view shot (POV) is the character's own eyes: we see what Rosie sees, and at most her own hands. Use POV when you want the viewer inside the character.

Sources: StudioBinder shot guide; Wikipedia (cowboy shot, Dutch angle, tilt); Veo prompt guide (angle terms).

Moves 1: turn the camera, or move it

There are many names for camera moves, but they fall into two families. This is the most useful idea in the module.

  • Turn in place. The camera stays at one point and only changes where it looks: pan (left or right), tilt (up or down) and roll (the Dutch angle).
  • Move. The camera itself travels: truck (sideways), pedestal (straight up or down), push-in and pull-out (towards or away from the subject; also called dolly in and dolly out), orbit or arc (around the subject), crane or boom (up on an arm) and tracking (following a moving subject).

You can see the difference in parallax: when the camera moves, near things slide past far things. When it only turns, everything moves together like one flat picture. Parallax is what makes a moving shot feel three-dimensional. Watch the bench in the front and the chalkboard at the back. Moves as camera values

In code-rendered 3D, a camera is three values. The position is where the camera is. The target is the point it looks at (three.js points the camera with lookAt). The field of view (FOV) is how wide it sees; in three.js, fov is the vertical angle in degrees. Every move is a recipe for these three values over time:

The lookAt trap

Code often calls camera.lookAt(subject) on every frame, so the camera always aims at the subject. Then a sideways move is not a truck: the camera slides and turns, so the subject stays in the centre and the room swings behind it. That can look good, but it is a truck plus a pan. For a pure truck, ask Claude to move the target by the same amount as the position.

A note on words. Crews use “dolly” for the wheeled cart, and also for any move on it. Some say “track left” where others say “truck left”. The AI video guides agree on one set, and so does this module: push in and pull out for towards and away, truck left or right for sideways, and tracking for following a subject.

Three more moves. Handheld is a small, shaky motion with a documentary feel. In 3D it is camera shake: noise added to the position and rotation. Remotion's noise2D gives the same noise for the same frame, so the shake stays repeatable, and Unreal has ready-made shake patterns. A whip pan is a pan so fast that the picture blurs. It is a strong transition: whip out of one shot and into the next. This is the “quick pan to Rosie” from the review notes. Last, a locked-off (static) shot does not move at all. That is a choice too, and often the right one.

Sources: Wikipedia (tracking shot); StudioBinder camera movements; three.js Object3D.lookAt; Veo, Kling and MiniMax guides (move terms); Unreal camera shakes; Remotion noise2D.

Moves 2: a zoom is not a push-in

One of the requests in the review notes was “zoom shot to the spinning globe”. That sounds clear, but it is the most common mix-up in this module. On a real camera, a zoom changes the lens's focal length. In 3D, it narrows or widens the FOV. Either way, the camera does not move. A push-in moves the camera closer. Both make the subject bigger, but they do not look the same.

A zoom magnifies everything by the same amount, so it is really a crop of the first frame. The perspective does not change. A push-in changes the perspective: the subject grows faster than the background, and near things slide out of the frame. That is why a push-in feels like going towards something, and a zoom feels like looking harder at it.

Usually, “zoom to the globe” means “get closer to the globe”, which is a push-in. When the difference matters, Claude should ask: zoom or push in? Better still, say it in your note.

Three famous variations

  • Dolly zoom (the “Vertigo effect”): the camera moves back while the lens zooms in, or the reverse. The subject stays the same size, and the room seems to stretch or shrink behind it. It is a strong effect for a moment of shock or realisation. Use it once.
  • Crash zoom (snap zoom): a very fast zoom. It is punchy and often comic. “Close and then quick zoom out” in the review notes is a crash zoom out, or a fast pull-out if the camera should move.
  • Reveal: a move that starts close or blocked and ends on something new. A pull-out from Rosie's pumpkin to the whole class is a reveal.
“Zoom” in AI prompts

Veo's guide defines a zoom correctly: the focal length changes and the camera does not move. Even so, video generators often turn “zoom in” into a push-in. MiniMax has separate commands, [Zoom in] and [Push in]. When the difference matters, describe it: “the camera moves closer to her”.

Sources: three.js PerspectiveCamera (zoom and fov); Wikipedia (zooming, perspective distortion, dolly zoom); Veo and MiniMax guides; field notes.

Lens, focus and depth

Photographers talk about lenses in millimetres; 3D code talks about the field of view in degrees. They describe the same thing. The focal length of a lens and the size of the camera's sensor together set the field of view. A long lens (85 mm) sees a narrow slice of the world; this is a telephoto. A short lens (24 mm) sees a wide view.

The three.js numbers come from its own formula, with its default 35 mm film gauge. The default fov of 50 at 16:9 is a wide lens, about 21 mm. three.js can also set the FOV from millimetres: camera.setFocalLength(85). Blender takes a focal length or an FOV, and Unreal's Cine Camera takes a sensor size and a focal length. So “an 85 mm look” is a fair request in every tool.

“Compression” comes from distance

People say a telephoto lens “compresses” space: the background looks big and close behind the subject. The American Society of Cinematographers (ASC) explains what really happens. The lens does not cause it. The distance does. From far away, Ada and the board are at more similar distances from the camera, so they look more similar in size. To keep Ada the same size from far away, you need a narrow FOV, and that is why people blame the lens.

So ask for “camera far back, long lens” (or “narrow FOV”) when you want the background big and flat behind the subject. Ask for “close, wide lens” when you want depth and space. A zoom from the same spot does not do either: it only crops. Veo's guide still says a telephoto lens “compresses distance”. As a prompt word, that is fine.

Focus and depth of field

Depth of field is the range of distances that look sharp. With a shallow depth of field, the subject is sharp and the background melts into soft blur (bokeh): the classic “cinematic” portrait. With deep focus, everything is sharp. On a real camera, a lower f-stop gives a shallower depth of field. A rack focus (or pull focus) moves the focus from one subject to another during the shot, for example from Rosie's pumpkin to Ada behind her.

Here is a catch for code-rendered 3D. A three.js camera has no depth of field: everything is sharp. Blur is a post effect (BokehPass, with a focus distance and an aperture). Blender and Unreal cameras do have focus distance and f-stop settings. So in 3D a rack focus is possible, but it costs work and render time. Ask for it on purpose.

Shorts: 9:16 is not just a taller frame

YouTube's player is 16:9. Shorts are vertical or square and can be up to 3 minutes long. When Claude makes a Short from a 16:9 three.js scene, watch one detail: three.js keeps the vertical angle. With the same fov, a tall 9:16 frame sees only a thin slice of the scene.

Re-frame each shot for the tall format, or ask Claude to keep the same horizontal view. YouTube's Shorts editor also marks a “non-safe” area near the edges, where its buttons sit. Keep faces and text away from the edges. YouTube gives no exact numbers for this area.

Composition and continuity

Composition is where things sit in the frame. Three habits cover most frames:

  • Rule of thirds. Imagine two lines across and two lines down. Put the subject on a line or where two lines cross, not in the dead centre.
  • Headroom. Leave a little space above the head, and put the eyes near the top third line. Too much space makes the subject sink; too little cuts them off.
  • Lead room (or look room). Leave space in the direction the subject looks or moves. Without it, the subject seems to press against the edge.

Centre framing is a valid choice too. A perfectly centred, symmetrical frame feels formal, calm or funny, and some directors build a whole style on it. Choose it on purpose; do not drift into it.

The 180-degree rule

When two people face each other, imagine a line through them. Keep every camera on one side of that line. Then Ada stays on the left and looks right, and Rosie stays on the right and looks left, in every shot. If one camera crosses the line, the two seem to swap places, and the viewer loses track of the scene for a moment.

This is a guideline, not a law. You can cross the line on purpose, for example with a camera that moves across it during a shot, so the viewer sees it happen. In code-rendered 3D, it is easy to break the rule by accident, because a virtual camera can go anywhere. Tell Claude: “keep the 180 line between Ada and Rosie”.

The 30-degree rule and jump cuts

When you cut between two shots of the same subject, change the angle by at least about 30 degrees, or change the shot size clearly. If you do not, the cut looks like a jump cut: the subject twitches in place. Vlogs use jump cuts on purpose, to remove pauses. Again, this is a guideline.

Coverage

Coverage is the set of shots that lets an editor choose. The classic plan starts wide and moves closer: first a master shot of the whole action, then mediums, close-ups and inserts. With a master, the editor can always cut back to the whole picture. For a lip-synced music video, one practical guide suggests a full-song pass for each setup (wide, medium, close), so every line exists in three sizes. It also says: frame a little wider than you need, because you can crop in later but never out. That guide is a vendor blog, so take it as practice, not research.

In code-rendered 3D, coverage is cheap: the same animation can render from several cameras. Ask Claude for alternatives. With an AI generator, each clip is one shot, so coverage means generating several clips.

Cut, or move?

The review notes said “cut to the blackboard” in one place and “zoom to the globe” in another. A hard cut switches to the next shot instantly. A camera move travels there continuously. In 3D, a hard cut means switching to another camera, or jumping the position and target in one frame. When a note could mean either, say which one you want.

Sources: Wikipedia (rule of thirds, headroom, lead room, 180-degree rule, 30-degree rule, jump cut); StudioBinder coverage guide; Pixo music-video playbook; field notes.

Time: holds, easing and slow motion

A great frame can still feel wrong if its timing is wrong. Timing has its own words.

A hold is the time a shot stays on screen. The tail (or button) is a final hold at the end of a scene or a song. “Hold the last scene for a couple more seconds” from the review notes is a request for a longer tail. Say holds in seconds, or in the music's terms: “hold for the whole phrase”, “cut on ‘gravity’”.

Easing: how a move starts and stops

In 3D animation, a move is a set of keyframes: you set the camera position at frame 0 and at frame 90, and the software fills in the frames between. How it fills them is the easing. With linear easing, the camera goes from standing still to full speed at once, and stops dead at the end. Blender's manual says it well: linear “prevents abrupt changes in value but not in speed”. Ease in starts slowly and ease out ends slowly. A spring is easing that imitates physics: in Remotion, its default settings overshoot a little and settle.

The review notes said the camera felt “jittery” and “bouncy”. Un-eased motion is a common cause. Ask for “ease in and out on every camera move”, or “a spring with no overshoot” if you like the quick start.

Every value comes from the frame number

Remotion renders a video in several browser tabs that share no state. Every animated value must come from useCurrentFrame(). A CSS animation or a timer looks fine in the preview, then flickers in the final render. If a render flickers but the preview is fine, this is the first thing to check. (The clips on this page follow the same rule: every frame is a function of time alone.)

Slow motion and speed ramps

A real camera makes slow motion by recording at a higher frame rate (60 or 120 frames per second) and playing the frames back at 24 or 30. In code-rendered 3D, you render time instead of recording it. Slow motion means slowing the scene's clock; this is a time remap. Blender calls it Time Stretching. A speed ramp changes the speed during the shot, for example from normal to a quarter speed and back.

Under a song, there is a catch. The song keeps real time: the music and the lip sync cannot slow down. So only the scene slows, and the cut points stay on the song's timeline. The slow part borrows time, so the shot ramps back to normal speed to land its cut on the beat. You will see this at the end of the clip in the next section. Live shoots solve the same problem another way: they play the track faster on set while the singer mimes, and slow the footage later. One practitioner describes playback at 250% for 60 fps footage slowed to 24, and says singing at that speed is “very very difficult”.

One more word: shutter angle. A film camera's shutter is open for part of each frame. A 180-degree shutter is open for half of each frame, which is the film norm. At 30 fps that is 1/60 of a second. It sets how much motion blur a moving thing gets. Remotion's CameraMotionBlur uses 180 degrees by default. A narrow angle gives crisp, choppy motion, a look that war and action films sometimes use.

Sources: Blender manual (interpolation, time stretching, motion blur); Remotion docs (fundamentals, interpolate, Easing, spring, CameraMotionBlur, flickering); Wikipedia (shutter angle); DV Info Net forum (live lip-sync slow motion); field notes.

Taste: how long a shot lasts, and what looks good

You feel it when a video is good. Much of that feeling has names, and some of it has numbers. This section collects both. The numbers come from measurements. The habits are craft advice that editors share widely; they are taste, so treat them as starting points.

How long should a shot last?

Researchers measured the shots in 160 Hollywood films from 1935 to 2010. The average shot length was about 10 seconds in the 1930s and 1940s, and fell below 4 seconds after 2000. In recent films, shorter shots also carry more motion. Interestingly, the share of shots with a moving camera went down (from 16% in 1959 to 6% in 1999): the extra motion comes from what happens in the frame, not from the camera.

Shot length also follows shot size. A wide shot needs time, because the eye scans a lot of detail. A close-up is read at once, so it can be cut shorter. An editing guide said so in 1944, and a 2017 study of one pop music video found the same pattern: the closer the framing, the shorter the shot. Its typical shot lasted about 2 seconds.

What we tend to find engaging

These habits explain much of what “looks good”. Each one is craft advice, not a law:

  • Vary the size. Make each shot a different size from the one before: wide, medium, close, insert. Two similar shots in a row look like a mistake.
  • Use contrast. Long against short, still against moving, wide against close. A fast run hits harder after a long hold.
  • One idea per shot. Each shot shows one thing. When the viewer has it, cut.
  • Give the eye one place to land. Put the subject of the next shot near where the eye already is. The editor Walter Murch calls this eye trace.
  • Move with a reason. Follow an action, reveal something or build a feeling, like a slow push-in as Rosie realises something. A move with no reason makes a video busy.
  • Hold on the peak. Give the emotional high point time. Do not cut away from the best moment.
  • Cut on action. A cut in the middle of a movement hides itself. The viewer follows the motion and does not notice the cut.

Walter Murch, an Oscar-winning editor, ranks what makes a good cut: emotion first, then story, then rhythm, then eye trace, then the two continuity rules (the flat screen and the 3D space). He gives emotion more than half of the weight. So a cut that breaks the 180-degree rule but carries the feeling beats a perfect cut that feels like nothing.

YouTube adds its own pressure. In YouTube Studio, the audience retention report shows the share of viewers still watching after 30 seconds, and the “dips” where people leave or skip. YouTube's advice: if your best moments come late, move them earlier. Your strongest shot can open the video.

Turn a feeling into a note

“It feels static” usually means the holds are too long or the sizes repeat: “cut at the phrase, then an MCU, then an insert”. “It feels busy” usually means too many moves or too many cuts: “lock off the camera in the verse; hold each shot for the whole line”.

Now put it together. A shot list is a table with one row per shot. Each row says the size and angle, the move, the subject and action, the length, how the shot starts and ends, and why it is there. For code-rendered 3D

  • Name the move with the terms from this module, or give numbers: “from 6 m to 2 m”, “fov 30”, “an 85 mm look”.
  • Say the easing (“ease in and out”) and the length, in seconds, beats or lyrics.
  • Say “hard cut” or “move” for every change of shot.
  • Name the rules you want kept: “keep the 180 line”, “eyes on the top third”.
  • Ask for still frames of each shot before a long render. Coverage is cheap, so ask for alternatives too.

For AI video generators

The official prompting guides from Google (Veo), Runway and Kling agree on a short list, and OpenAI's archived Sora guide said the same:

  • Be plain and specific. Shot size, angle and move. Sora's guide called “cinematic look” a weak cue and “wide shot, low angle” a strong one. Kling calls “make the camera cool” too unclear.
  • One camera move per shot. Sora: “one clear camera move and one clear subject action”. Kling warns that contradictory cues, such as a static camera with a fast push-in, confuse the model.
  • Say what you want, not what you do not want. Write “static camera” rather than “no shake”.
  • Start simple and add one element at a time. Shorter clips follow instructions better, and results vary from run to run, so make several.
  • One generation is one shot. A cut inside one prompt rarely works. Cut in the edit.

Vendor details: Runway suggests “The camera [motion] as the subject [action]” for an image-to-video prompt. Its guide also warns that even “the locked-off camera remains perfectly still” may not give a perfectly still shot. MiniMax uses bracket commands such as [Push in] and [Truck left]. Veo's guide says some “advanced” angles and lenses are not officially supported. OpenAI removed Sora 2 and its video API on 24 September 2026, so treat its guide as past advice.
