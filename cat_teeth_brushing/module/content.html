Meet Oreo

This is Oreo. Oreo is an adult tuxedo cat with 30 teeth and firm views about all of them. Oreo does not want a toothbrush in his mouth. Maybe your cat agrees with him.

This module is for that cat: an adult who already objects. It does not say "start when they're kittens". That ship has sailed, and it had claws.

The plan is slow and cheap: tiny steps, paid in chicken-flavoured paste, over weeks. You will learn to stop before Oreo has to tell you to. Nobody gets hurt, including you.

Not vet advice

This is general education from veterinary sources (listed at the end). It does not replace your vet. If your cat's mouth looks sore, or your cat is in distress, see your vet first.

Why bother?

Because Oreo's mouth is probably not as fine as he says. Most adult cats have some dental disease. The figures depend on the source and the age group: from about half to about 90% of cats older than 3 or 4 years.

It starts with plaque: a soft film of bacteria, saliva and food on the teeth. A brush wipes it off. Left alone, it collects at the gumline, where the gum meets the tooth. There it irritates the gums (gingivitis). Mild gingivitis usually goes away with brushing.

Within about 36-48 hours, plaque starts to harden into tartar. Tartar is the yellow-brown crust you see on old teeth. A brush cannot remove it. Untreated, gum disease goes deeper (periodontitis), and that damage eventually cannot be reversed.

Two more facts. First, brushing is the "gold standard" of home care, but it is not magic. Tooth resorption, a painful condition common in cats, has no known cause. None of our sources say that brushing prevents it. Second, Oreo will not tell you when it hurts.

Cats hide pain. A cat can eat well, have normal breath and still have severe dental disease. So the vet sees inside Oreo's mouth at least once a year, and every 3-6 months if he has dental problems.

Bad breath, by the way, is usually gum disease. But kidney disease, diabetes and liver disease can cause it too. Persistent bad breath is a vet visit, not a toothpaste problem.

Sources: iCatCare, Cornell Feline Health Center, VCA, VOHC.

Attitude, or a sore mouth?

Here is the most important idea in this module. A cat that hisses when you touch his mouth may not be "difficult". He may be in pain.

Irritability is a listed sign of mouth pain and of tooth resorption. The 2022 cat handling guidelines from the AAFP and ISFM (two feline vet organisations) tell vets to consider pain in any cat that is hard to handle. They name gum disease as a common chronic pain. If touching the mouth hurts, no amount of chicken paste will fix that.

So before any training, book a vet exam. Look out for these signs, and tell the vet about them:

  • dropping food, chewing on one side, or swallowing kibble without chewing;
  • pawing at the mouth, drooling, head shaking;
  • red, sore or bleeding gums, visible tartar, loose or broken teeth;
  • refusing dry food, or a sudden preference for soft food;
  • bad breath, weight loss, less grooming, more grumpiness.

If the vet finds tartar or sore gums, the order is: cleaning first, then let the mouth heal, then start training. Brushing sore gums hurts, and it teaches Oreo that brushes hurt.

Get help for a cat in distress

The same guidelines say owners should not train cats that show distress, or that have behaviour problems, on their own. For those cats, training should happen "only with the input of a suitably qualified feline behaviorist". If that sounds like Oreo, ask your vet for a referral. This is not failure; it is the plan.

Sources: Cornell, iCatCare, VCA, AAFP/ISFM 2022 guidelines.

Reading Oreo: stop at the quiet signals

Oreo talks all the time. Mostly he whispers. The trick is to listen before he has to shout.

The AAFP/ISFM guidelines describe three fear responses, in order. Each one comes when the one before it did not work:

  1. Quiet (inhibition). He freezes. His ears turn sideways, his lips go tight, his pupils get big. This stage is "often not recognized". A still cat is not a calm cat.
  2. Away (avoidance). He turns his head away, leans away, tries to leave.
  3. Loud (repelling). Growl, hiss, swat, bite.

The bite is the last stage, not the first. Don't wait for stage 3. Stop at stage 1: brush away, treat, and next time start with an easier step.

Some things never help, however tempting:

  • No scruffing. The guidelines say scruffing is "no longer recommended for cats at any life stage". It causes fear that the cat remembers.
  • No tight restraint. Restraint increases fear and frustration, and frustration brings faster, stronger reactions. A towel is fine as a loose wrap that feels safe, not as a straitjacket.
  • Oreo can always leave. "Releasing the cat must always be an option." If he leaves, the session is over. That is information, not defeat.

Sources: AAFP/ISFM 2022 guidelines, VCA, iCatCare.

The slow ladder

Vets call it desensitisation and counter-conditioning. In plain words: break the job into tiny steps, and pay for each step with something Oreo loves. Each step should be easy. If he is unsure, go back to an easier one. As VCA puts it: "your pet gets to set the pace".

One common ladder (the sources differ in the details):

  1. Touch and stroke his face, where he already likes it. Treat.
  2. Let him lick cat toothpaste off your finger. Try other flavours if he is not keen.
  3. Lift his lip for a moment, mouth closed. Treat.
  4. Touch one canine tooth with the brush.
  5. Brush a few teeth, a second or two at a time, with a treat after each.
  6. Brush one whole side for 10 seconds, then both sides. Build up to about 30 seconds per side.

Some practical rules:

  • Short sessions. About 30 seconds at the start. Stop while it is still going well.
  • Same time, same place. A quiet time, when he is calm. Cats like routine.
  • Always end on a treat. Even after a bad session: especially then.
  • Practise the head hold at other times, not only when the brush is out.
  • Expect setbacks. Things may go backwards when the brush first appears.

How long does it take? For some cats, days. For an adult who objects, expect weeks or months. iCatCare: "it doesn't matter how long it takes". If there is no progress after a few weeks, VCA says to get help from a behaviour professional.

For a hostile cat

Start lower than any ladder shows: with touch he already enjoys. No published ladder was made for hostile cats. This one follows the same principle: tiny steps, each paid for. A behaviourist can design a ladder for your cat.

Sources: iCatCare, Cornell, VCA, WSAVA dental committee, AAFP/ISFM 2022.

Kit and technique, fingers out of the mouth

Toothpaste: cat toothpaste only. It does not foam, and it is safe to swallow. Human toothpaste is unsafe for cats, and so is baking soda. Flavours include chicken, beef and fish.

Brush: a small, soft cat toothbrush with a handle. A child's soft toothbrush also works. Use one brush per cat, and replace it every 3 months.

Finger brushes: sources differ. VCA and WSAVA list them as options. iCatCare says to use them with caution, and to avoid them in most cases, because of the bite risk. For a cat like Oreo, keep your fingers out of his mouth: use the handle.

Now watch the whole thing, from setup to treat:

  1. Set up. A quiet, small room with the door closed. A towel on a low table, or your lap. Paste on the brush, treats ready.
  2. Position. Oreo sits with his back to you. It is less confrontational, and if he wriggles, he backs into you.
  3. Head hold. Palm on top of his head, towards the back. Thumb and second finger on his cheekbones. Index finger lifted, away from his eyes.
  4. Lift the lip. His mouth stays closed. Lift the upper lip with your thumb. Never force the mouth open.
  5. Angle. Bristles at 45 degrees to the tooth, aimed into the gap between tooth and gum.
  6. Brush. Small, gentle circles along the gumline, mainly the big cheek teeth and the canines. Start with 10 seconds per side; build up to about 30.
  7. Read him. Quiet signals? Stop. "Just one more tooth" is how fingers get bitten.
  8. Pay up. Treat, praise, done.

Brush the outside surfaces: that is where most damage happens. Cornell and VCA say the tongue cleans the inside surfaces well. The WSAVA dental committee says to brush the inside "if possible". With Oreo, it is not possible, and that is fine.

A little bleeding from the gums at first is common, and it stops as the gums get healthier. If they bleed more than that, tell your vet.

How often?

Daily is ideal: plaque starts to harden within about two days. VCA calls three times a week the minimum. Sources differ; aim for daily, and accept what Oreo allows.

Sources: iCatCare, Cornell, VCA, WSAVA dental committee.

Keeping all your fingers

Everything in this module is also bite prevention. The habits that keep you safe are the same ones that make training work:

  • Handle, not finger. A brush with a handle keeps your fingers out of his mouth.
  • Mouth closed. Lift the lip only. Never force the mouth open.
  • Stop at the quiet signals, not at the hiss.
  • No scruffing, no tight restraint. Both make the next session worse.
  • He can always leave. A cat with a way out does not need to fight.

Even the AVDC (the American Veterinary Dental College) notes that people working on an awake animal's mouth may be bitten when the animal reacts. Respect the teeth you are cleaning.

If you are bitten or scratched

End the session. Wash the wound well with soap and water. If a bite breaks the skin, contact a doctor. This is general first aid, not medical advice.

Sources: iCatCare, VCA, AAFP/ISFM 2022, AVDC.

Plan B: when the brush never works

iCatCare is honest about it: "Some cats simply won't tolerate having their teeth brushed, no matter how much you persevere." If that is Oreo, you have not failed. You move to Plan B.

The VOHC seal. The Veterinary Oral Health Council gives a seal to dental diets, treats, water additives and other products. It means the maker's trials met the VOHC standard for plaque control, tartar control, or both. VOHC does not test products itself: it reviews the maker's data. Check which claim the seal covers, and ask your vet which product suits your cat. VOHC publishes the accepted list on its website (last updated August 2026).

Honest expectations. Brushing stays the gold standard. iCatCare says dental chews "help to reduce (but not prevent)" plaque and tartar. Our sources do not say how much less these products do than a brush.

"Anaesthesia-free" cleaning is not Plan B

Some places offer to scale a cat's teeth while it is awake. The AVDC discourages this. It cannot reach below the gumline, where the disease is, and it cannot examine the whole mouth. The effect is "purely cosmetic" and gives "a false sense of accomplishment". Sharp tools near a moving head can also injure the cat, or the person. Anaesthesia is never 100% risk-free, but modern methods keep the risk low.

For the vet visits themselves, ask your vet about carrier training and, if needed, medication to reduce anxiety before visits.

Sources: iCatCare, VCA, VOHC, AVDC, AAFP/ISFM 2022.
